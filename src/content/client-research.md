<h2 id="start-with-a-reason-to-engage" data-index-label="Purpose">Start with a reason to engage</h2>

For an agency choosing which accounts to pursue, useful research explains account fit, supports a point of view, and identifies questions for the first conversation. This proposed workflow adapts the lab’s boutique-agency offering: start with ten candidate companies, select three together, and prepare three focused client conversations.

<h2 id="pilot-inputs" data-index-label="Inputs">Frame the agency’s market thesis</h2>

Start with the agency’s target market, services, commercial priorities, relevant experience, and capacity. Add account and buyer research only where it changes a decision. Keep the reason to pursue an account visible alongside the evidence and the unknowns.

The first output is a shortlist, not an outreach campaign. A small pilot establishes whether the research helps the agency choose better conversations.

<h2 id="workflow" data-index-label="Workflow">Workflow</h2>

1. **Frame ten candidates.** Agree on market, account criteria, exclusions, and the services the agency can deliver. Capture each candidate’s account signals and likely buyer context.
2. **Compare and choose three.** Gather public company, competitor, and buyer information. Link observations to sources, separate facts from hypotheses, and review the shortlist with the agency.
3. **Go deep on the selected accounts.** Prepare a point of view, likely buying roles, message options, relevant agency proof, and questions to test the value hypothesis.
4. **Enter the conversation and learn.** The agency approves the package and leads the conversation. Record objections, corrections, and next actions so the next shortlist starts with better context.

<h2 id="selection-scorecard" data-index-label="Selection">A scorecard that supports a decision</h2>

Use a short qualitative review instead of a precise-looking score without evidence:

- **Account fit:** Does the company match the agency’s market and service strengths?
- **Reason to act:** What observable change or business problem makes a conversation timely?
- **Buyer context:** Who owns the problem, and what would they need to agree on?
- **Relevant proof:** Which actual agency experience supports the proposed work?
- **Unknowns:** What needs to be resolved before a pitch is useful?

The agency can choose **research further**, **hold**, or **pass**, with a reason and next action attached to each decision.

### The selected account

**Synthetic scenario: a retailer expanding its online storefront.** The company and expansion are invented for this example.

> **Account signal:** Storefront expansion.
>
> **Hypothesis:** The team may need a clearer connection between acquisition channels and repeat purchases.
>
> **Discovery question:** How do you connect campaign reporting with repeat-purchase behavior today?
>
> **Suggested first step:** Map the existing measurement approach and choose one gap to investigate.

**Illustrative opening message**

> Your storefront expansion suggests a useful first conversation: how are you connecting acquisition spend with repeat purchases? We could start by mapping the current measurement approach and identifying one question worth testing.

The account brief provides the reasoning behind the message. A real package would attach public sources and relevant agency experience to support that reasoning.

<h2 id="pitch-package" data-index-label="Pitch package">What each selected account receives</h2>

A useful package holds the account brief, buyer context, value hypothesis, message options, discovery questions, and next action together. It includes links to the supporting observations and the relevant agency work. A reviewer should be able to see why the proposed conversation fits this account.

<h2 id="after-the-pitch" data-index-label="Delivery loop">After the pitch: retain account learning</h2>

The older agency materials also describe an intelligence layer behind delivery. This is a possible extension of the acquisition workflow:

1. **Audit:** Combine approved analytics and campaign exports with tracking-health checks and competitor observations.
2. **Plan:** Compare spend scenarios with their assumptions, data gaps, and measurement approach.
3. **Review:** Prepare a weekly performance brief showing what changed, questions to investigate, and decisions for the strategist.
4. **Retain:** Carry approved decisions, experiments, objections, and account context into the next review.

### Reviewable delivery artifacts

The legacy campaign concept names six useful outputs: a **strategic brief** with audience and rationale; a **creative brief** with goals, tone, and deliverables; a **media plan** with channel assumptions and proposed spend; a **client presentation** connecting the recommendation to evidence; a **performance report** comparing observations with the baseline; and a **decision journal** linking a decision to its owner, reasoning, and outcome.

A fictional journal entry might read: “Test repeat-purchase measurement before increasing spend. Reason: the acquisition report omits returning customers. Owner: account strategist. Next review: compare the joined records and revise the plan.” This preserves the reasoning that a later brief needs.

The strategist owns campaign direction, client commitments, and changes to spend. A brief is useful only when its inputs and assumptions can be inspected. This extension describes a workflow to explore; the website does not connect to ad platforms or operate campaigns.

<h2 id="agency-ownership" data-index-label="Human review">Agency review</h2>

The agency verifies the account signal, challenges the hypothesis, and checks that the proposed work matches its expertise. It approves the message and leads the client relationship.
